# Genocide


## Legal Definitions


### Article 6 of the Rome Statute
Genocide is legally defined in article 6 of the Rome Statute[^1] as such:

> For the purpose of this Statute, “genocide” means any of the following acts committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in, part a national, ethnical, racial or religious group, as such:
> (a) Killing members of the group;
> (b) Causing serious bodily or mental harm to members of the group;
> (c) Deliberately inflicting on the group conditions of life calculated to bring about its physical destruction in whole or in part;
> (d) Imposing measures intended to prevent births within the group;
> (e) Forcibly transferring children of the group to another group.

The Rome Statute is significant as it is the governing text of the International Criminal Court.

### The Genocide Convention
The Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide[^2] defines genocide in article 2 as such:

> In the present Convention, genocide means any of the following acts committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group, as such:
> (a) Killing members of the group;
> (b) Causing serious bodily or mental harm to members of the group;
> (c) Deliberately inflicting on the group conditions of life calculated to bring about its physical destruction in whole or in part;
> (d) Imposing measures intended to prevent births within the group;
> (e) Forcibly transferring children of the group to another group.

## Scholarly Discussion

### We Charge Genocide
Shortly after the introduction of laws prohibiting genocide, African-Americans had noticed the similarities in treatment of African-Americans by the US Government and institutions and the definition of genocide. As a result, the Civil Rights Congress[^3] published a book titled _We Charge Genocide: The Crime of Government Against the Negro People_[^4]. The initial basis for this claim was that, over a six year period, there had been 151 killings of black people in the Eastern United States from as far west as Dallas and as far east as the State of New York[^5].

### Argument for expansion of the enumerated grounds
While the legal definition of genocide only enumerates four grounds (national, ethnical, racial or religious), there is an argument for expansion of the enumerated grounds, given the more expanse grounds defined in the Refugee Convention, which holds that a refugee is a person persecuted on the basis of "race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group and political opinion". It would also be more in line with the much more expansive definition of groups covered under the definition of Crimes Against Humanity in article 7 of the Rome Statute[^1], which includes "any civilian population". Scholars have noted that this omission can only offer "partial redress" "given the evolving nature of identity-based violence"[^6]
